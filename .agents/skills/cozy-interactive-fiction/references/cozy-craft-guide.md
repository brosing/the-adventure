# Cozy Interactive Fiction — Craft Guide

Genre and prose principles distilled from editorial craft guidance (cozy fantasy conventions, personification, character introduction, climax construction, and plot-hole avoidance), adapted for branching narrative.

---

## 1. The five cozy fantasy promises (every branch must keep all five)

1. **Low stakes.** Conflicts are personal: keeping a shop afloat, earning a place in a village, mending a friendship, solving a gentle local mystery. Low stakes ≠ no stakes — the outcome must matter deeply *to the protagonist* (if the tea shop fails, she goes back to a life she fled). Never lethal, never gruesome, never world-ending.
2. **Lighthearted whimsy.** Enchanted homes, talking creatures, temperamental magical objects, tea-growing dragons; quirky humor, playful banter, magical mishaps played for charm. Whimsy is woven into scenes, never dumped as exposition.
3. **Growth and healing.** The real plot is internal: recovering from old hurts, discovering who you are, finding belonging — unfolding gently, because of the journey. Show growth in small accumulating moments across scenes, not one announced epiphany.
4. **Community.** A warm, quirky ensemble actively drives the protagonist's growth. In IF this means recurring NPCs who remember the player's choices. No one is ever truly alone; long solitary stretches are off-genre.
5. **Happy ending — on every route.** All branches converge on warmth: struggles resolved, bonds strengthened, the world brighter. Different routes can reach *different* happy endings, but none may end sad, and any bittersweetness must resolve into clear hope.

Underneath the coziness it's still fantasy: build a real setting and a magic system with visible rules, limits, and costs — then obey them everywhere (see §5).

## 2. Prose techniques for cozy atmosphere

**Personification is the cozy engine.** Give the world gentle agency so the setting itself feels like a companion:
- Active verbs with intent beat passive description: not "steam rose from the kettle" but "the kettle sighed contentedly." Not "the fire was warm" but "the fire stretched and settled like a cat."
- Personify the *home space* as a character — a cottage that leans in to listen, shelves that tidy themselves when pleased, a door that sticks when it sulks. This is the cozy version of the setting-as-participant technique.
- **Pathetic fallacy, warm edition:** let weather and setting mirror the protagonist's inner state — rain tapping companionably during a reflective scene, sunlight pooling on the counter when hope returns. Use it to *replace* naming the emotion, not to double it.
- Personify emotions as gentle physical forces: "contentment settled over her shoulders like a shawl," "doubt tugged at his sleeve."
- **Multi-sensory always:** sound (crackle, hum, purr), smell (cinnamon, rain, old paper), texture (wool, worn wood, warm ceramic), taste. Books aren't movies; smell and touch do the heavy cozy lifting.
- **Restraint.** If every object whispers and every breeze murmurs, the effect deadens into melodrama. Two or three touches of personification per scene, placed where mood matters most.

**Tone:** humor gentle and character-driven; sentences unhurried; verbs warm. Avoid clinical or ominous diction unless immediately, kindly subverted.

## 3. Introducing characters (players meet many NPCs — make each land)

Every significant NPC's first appearance should use 2–3 of these (never all — that's an info-dump):
- **One or two telling physical details** that characterize, not catalog (flour permanently in the eyebrows; a scarf knitted from mismatched yarn ends).
- **A quirk** that hints at inner life (a gnome who alphabetizes pebbles; a cat who only speaks in questions).
- **A distinct voice** — recognizable diction/rhythm without a dialogue tag.
- **Action-first entrance** — show them mid-doing-something-characteristic rather than describing them statically.
- **Worldbuilding melded in** — let their trade, home, or magic teach the world naturally.
- **A backstory hint** that raises a gentle question ("she got started late") — answered later as a reward, not dumped now.

In IF specifically: an NPC's quirk + voice is also a *consistency contract* — the player will meet them on multiple branches, and their personality must be identical everywhere unless growth is shown on-page.

## 4. Tension, choices, and the cozy climax

**Tension without peril.** Plot is a chain of questions asked and answered. Keep a small question open at all times (Will the mysterious customer return? Will the soufflé of truce work?), answer it, open another. Escalate by making things matter more emotionally, not by making them dangerous. Avoid false stakes: if a complication is announced, its resolution must visibly change something, or trust erodes.

**Choice design:**
- 2–4 options; each represents a different value or approach (bold / careful / kind / curious), not cosmetic variants of one act.
- No punishing outcomes. "Failure" = a charming complication, a detour, a different relationship blooming. Every route stays playable and warm.
- **Echoes make agency real.** Track choices in the story bible and pay them off visibly 1–3 scenes later: the NPC you helped vouches for you; the herb you chose flavors the festival tea. An unechoed choice was a fake choice.
- Occasionally include one whimsical wildcard option ("Ask the teapot for its opinion") — these delight cozy readers and showcase the world.

**The cozy climax:**
- Place it ~75–90% through the planned arc; keep everything after it brief.
- It must be *recognizable* as the turning point even though it's quiet — an opening day, a festival, a confession, a reconciliation, standing up gently for oneself. (A hero's climax can be as undramatic as deciding to open a bakery with a best friend — that IS the showdown here.)
- It answers the story's big question, and internal + external resolve together: the external moment (the shop succeeds) must *demonstrate* the internal growth (she finally believes she belongs).
- **Earn it.** Seed 2–3 small details early — an offhand skill, a planted herb, a shy NPC's talent — that combine at the climax. In branching structure, plant seeds in shared/early passages so every route has them available. Clues should feel like ordinary charm in the moment and click in retrospect.
- Genre-consistent tone: the climax feels warm and emotionally full, never violent or terrifying.

## 5. Plot-hole prevention across branches (the story bible)

Branching fiction multiplies plot-hole risk: what one branch establishes, another can contradict. Maintain a story bible from scene one and check every new passage against it. Track:

- **World & magic rules** (the highest-risk area): every cost, limit, and mechanism shown. Magic must never become a free plot-convenience — problems are solved by effort, cleverness, or kindness *within* the rules. Any exception needs clear, credible on-page motivation.
- **Continuity facts:** appearances, possessions, layout of the village, timeline, weather, what each NPC knows. Small slips (the blue door turning red) erode trust even when the plot survives; the warmer and more sincere the story, the more they jar.
- **Character contracts:** each character's core traits ranked by importance. The more central a trait, the more on-page pressure required before they act against it. Plot-convenient betrayals of character are the most common branching failure — if a branch needs the gruff blacksmith to suddenly gush, either seed it or redesign the branch.
- **Player state:** choices made, relationships built, items/skills earned — both to echo them (§4) and to avoid contradicting them ("you've never been to the mill" when the player chose to visit it).

**Three hole types to self-audit for, in severity order:** logical inconsistency (breaks the story's rules — fix immediately), continuity error (contradicts a detail — fix before it compounds), out-of-character moment (unjustified behavior — justify on-page or rewrite).
