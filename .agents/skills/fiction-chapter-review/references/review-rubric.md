# Review Rubric — Five Semantic Lenses

Craft criteria distilled from editorial best practices (Reedsy craft guides on personification, plot holes, character introductions, story climax, and cozy fantasy). Apply every lens to every chapter; apply the whole-story checks at the end.

---

## Lens 1 — Figurative language & personification

Personification gives human qualities (will, emotion, intent) to non-human things: nature, weather, emotions, abstractions (time, death, fate), animals, machines, body parts, everyday objects.

**What good looks like:**
- **Active verbs with intent, not passive motion.** "The ocean swallowed the shoreline in eager gulps" vs. "the waves lap the shore." The first implies appetite and will; the second is inert description.
- **Pathetic fallacy that earns its keep.** Personified setting/weather mirroring a character's inner state ("the sky tore open and the wind howled") is powerful when it replaces telling ("her grief was terrible") — flag when the author does both, which is redundant.
- **Setting as participant.** Landscapes and houses can reflect internal conflict AND external conflict simultaneously (the hostile moors of *Wuthering Heights*; the watchful architecture of Hill House). Reward this layering.
- **Multi-sensory grounding.** Personified settings should engage sound, smell, texture — not just visuals.
- **Emotions as forces.** "Grief clutched at her throat," "jealousy gnawed at him" — emotions given physical agency hit harder than named emotions. Guilt manifesting physically (Lady Macbeth's unwashable spot) is the gold standard.

**Failure patterns to flag:**
- Passive, will-less description where a scene needs atmosphere.
- **Melodrama from over-personification.** Restraint separates atmosphere from melodrama. If every breeze whispers and every shadow creeps, the effect deadens. Flag density.
- **Clichéd personification** used without freshness: time marching on, fate knocking, the wind whispering. Note when a fresher image would serve.
- **Unresearched nature-as-character.** If the natural setting acts as a character but its details ring false to the real place/ecosystem, readers will notice. Flag implausible flora/fauna/geography.
- Mixed or broken personification (an entity given contradictory agencies within a passage).

## Lens 2 — Plot integrity (the three plot-hole types)

A plot hole is an inconsistency that breaks the rules, logic, or timeline of a story. Classify every problem into one of three types, in descending severity:

### Type A — Logical inconsistency (most severe)
Something clashes with rules or events established earlier **in a way that makes the plot unworkable**.
- In-world rule violations: a vampire entering uninvited when the story established vampires can't (*Salem's Lot*). Magic systems are the biggest risk area — every use of magic must obey the costs, limits, and rules already shown.
- Real-world rule violations: premises built on legal/scientific misunderstandings (*Double Jeopardy*'s botched case law). The more the plot depends on the fact, the worse the error.
- **Review technique:** maintain a story bible while reading. Record every rule the moment it's established ("magic requires spoken words," "the shop can't afford repairs," "gnomes can't lie"), then audit every later scene against the list.

### Type B — Continuity error (moderate)
A detail contradicts an established detail, but the plot still works: eye color changing between chapters, a dead minor character reappearing (Pylaimenes in the *Iliad*), an object moving inexplicably.
- These don't unravel the plot but erode reader trust — and the more serious the story's tone, the more they irritate. A comedy can survive Jerry's inconsistently locked door; a mystery cannot survive a fingerprint from a gloved killer.
- **Review technique:** track physical descriptions, possessions, injuries, dates/timeline, weather, and who-knows-what in the story bible.

### Type C — Out-of-character moment (most forgivable — but flag it)
A character acts against their established nature, usually because the plot needs them to (L releasing Misa in *Death Note*; Mike abandoning Kaylee in *Breaking Bad*).
- One understandable blip under pressure is human. A decision *completely* at odds with a core trait, unjustified on the page, feels like betrayal.
- Rule of thumb: the more central a trait is to the character, the more narrative pressure is needed to justify acting against it. Ask: "did the plot force this, or did the character choose it?"
- **Distinguish from growth:** a change that's seeded, motivated, and acknowledged is a character arc, not a hole.

## Lens 3 — Character introductions & consistency

Evaluate each character's **first appearance** against these eight techniques. A strong intro uses 2–4 of them; using none produces a forgettable entrance; cramming all eight produces an info-dump.

1. **Telling physical details** — a few memorable, characterizing specifics (a tattoo edge, slumped shoulders "as though protecting something") beat exhaustive head-to-toe description.
2. **Quirks** — one or two out-of-the-ordinary behaviors that stick (L's squatting and nail-biting) and *signal* something about the character's inner life.
3. **Unique voice** — diction, rhythm, humor, and worldview that identify the character without a dialogue tag (Holden Caulfield's run-ons and cynicism).
4. **Dialogue behavior** — how they treat others in conversation: control, deflection, warmth, subtext (Lecter's first exchange with Clarice).
5. **Action-first entrances** — character revealed through what they *do* (Chigurh's escape) rather than narrated summary. Show, don't tell.
6. **Vivid, connotative word choice** — the adjectives around a character shape perception ("disagreeable-looking" Mary Lennox).
7. **Worldbuilding melded in** — the intro teaches us the world through the character (Rincewind's intro establishing the Discworld). Flag intros that pause the character to lecture about the world.
8. **Backstory hints** — small, relevant allusions that raise questions (Atticus "got started late") rather than flashback dumps. Only backstory relevant to the arc belongs.

**Also check:** Is the POV character the reader's emotional hook into the world, introduced before heavy worldbuilding? On later appearances, does the character's voice/quirk set stay consistent (feeds Lens 2, Type C)?

## Lens 4 — Tension & climax

**Chapter-level tension:** Every scene should be asking or answering a question. Plot is a chain of questions asked and answered — some resolved within a scene, some spanning chapters, one big one spanning the book. Per chapter, ask:
- What question does this chapter raise? What does it answer? (A chapter that does neither is a pacing dead spot.)
- Are stakes escalating? Techniques: make things emotionally/physically harder; strip away the character's escape options one by one; add cost to every choice.
- **False stakes check:** if a threat is announced but nothing changes whether it resolves or not, tension deflates and reader trust erodes. Stakes must connect to what the character most wants or fears.

**Whole-story climax check:**
- **Placement:** climax lands ~75–95% through (genre fiction especially); everything after should be brief. A long meandering post-climax section is a classic pacing flaw. (Exception: deliberate tragic structure with a midpoint point-of-no-return.)
- **Recognizability:** even a quiet climax must be clearly the turning point.
- **Answers the big question** raised at the start. If readers reach the falling action still confused, either the climax lacks clarity or the opening questions drifted.
- **Genre-consistent tone:** a thriller's climax feels urgent; a romance's, emotional; a cozy's, warm and personal (opening the bakery counts as a climax!).
- **Emotionally satisfying:** internal and external conflict should peak *together* — the external victory should demonstrate internal growth (Katniss's berries).
- **Earned:** twists and payoffs must be seeded with clues that read as ordinary details in the moment and click in retrospect (*The Sixth Sense*). An unseeded twist = cheated readers. During review, list the seeds; if you can't find any, flag it.
- **Climax types** for vocabulary when discussing: Hero's Climax (protagonist overcomes final hurdle), Action Climax (physical showdown + emotional payoff), Tragic Climax (midpoint point-of-no-return, downfall after), Surprise Climax (rug-pull that amplifies rather than undermines the A-story), Anti-Climax (deliberate denial of the expected payoff to redirect meaning — only works if purposeful).

## Lens 5 — Cozy fantasy genre promise

Apply strictly when the manuscript is cozy fantasy. The five elements, each scored in the genre scorecard:

1. **Low stakes.** Conflicts are personal and small-scale: running a business, mending a relationship, a local mystery, finding one's place. No life-or-death peril, gruesome events, or world-ending quests. Setbacks are real and meaningful but never crushing. Flag any scene where the danger turns lethal or the tone turns grim — that's a genre breach unless swiftly and warmly defused.
2. **Lighthearted & whimsical.** Fanciful elements (talking creatures, enchanted homes, tea-growing dragons, roaming castles) that add wonder without overcomplicating the plot; gentle or quirky humor; playful banter; magical mishaps played for charm. Whimsy should be woven in, not front-loaded exposition.
3. **Growth or healing focus.** The protagonist's journey is internal — overcoming past hurts, self-discovery, finding peace — unfolding *gently*. The healing should happen because of the journey, not despite it. Check that the growth is visible on the page in small accumulating moments, not announced in a single epiphany.
4. **Community.** A warm ensemble is a driving force in the protagonist's growth, not backdrop. Quirky but well-intentioned neighbors/friends/found family; the felt sense that no one is truly alone. Flag long solitary stretches — cozy protagonists heal *with* people.
5. **Happy ending.** Struggles overcome, relationships mended or strengthened, the world left brighter and more hopeful; the reader closes the book warm. Bittersweet is acceptable only if hope clearly wins.

Underneath the cozy layer it is still fantasy: fantastical setting, otherworldly elements, and a magic system that obeys its own rules (Lens 2 applies with full force — cozy readers still notice broken magic rules; magic as a free plot-convenience is a flaw even here).

**Cozy-specific tension note:** low stakes is not no stakes. The scorecard should confirm that outcomes still *matter to the character* (the shop failing means returning to a life of violence, à la *Legends & Lattes*) — that's how cozy sustains tension without peril.
