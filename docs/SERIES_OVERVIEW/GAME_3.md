# GAME III

## Working Title

The Girl Who Carried Time

### Theme

Presence

Mindfulness.

Being fully *here*, with *this* person, at *this* moment.

Not finishing objectives. Not getting through the day.

Here.

### Story Genre

Quiet Realism

Not the grand quest of the Listening Forest.

Not the ancient mystery of the Keeper.

An ordinary town. Ordinary people. The extraordinary difficulty of giving your full attention to any of them.

### Game Genre

Narrative Adventure

### Reading Level

Explorative

Game I taught the player to read attentively — stay with the story, notice what is quiet.

Game II taught the player to read connectedly — hold two things at once.

This game teaches the player to navigate — to choose what to attend to, knowing they cannot attend to everything.

---

### When

Between Game I and Game IV.

After the story of the boy spread.

People began building listening houses. Towns grew quieter at the edges. But the center — the market, the schedules, the running of things — grew faster.

Something new had appeared: the ability to be in two places in your mind at once.

Not peace. Not noise.

A third thing.

Distraction that wears the face of busyness.

---

### The World

A coastal town called Varemoor.

Not Bellwood. Smaller. Older. Quieter in the way that old grief is quiet.

It has a lighthouse.

It has a bakery.

It has a library.

A station — not for trains, but for the small ferry that connects the island to the mainland, twice a day.

A tea house above the harbor.

These places existed long before Game I.

They will be slightly changed by the time Game IV arrives.

The player is the first person to visit all of them in one game.

---

### The Protagonist

Her name is Maren.

She is not young. Somewhere between her first life and her second.

She arrived in Varemoor six months ago to care for an elderly aunt.

The aunt is mostly recovered.

Maren is still here.

She does not know why.

She used to know what she was doing next. Every day was a schedule. Every hour had a purpose. She was excellent at being in the right place. She had never learned to be in a place.

Now she has an ordinary life for the first time in a long time.

She finds it unbearable in a way she cannot name.

---

### The Mechanics

Everything from Game II:

- Examine
- Notebook
- Dialogue history

New:

- **NPC schedules**
- **Presence meter** (internal, never shown as a number)
- **Journal**
- **Side conversations** (optional, never blocking)
- **Relationship depth** (changes what people tell you, not what you can do)
- **Small inventory** (letters, objects with stories, not tools)

Still story-driven.

---

### The Central Mechanic: Schedules That Listen

Every person in Varemoor has a life.

The lighthouse keeper reads in the morning and works at night.

The baker is loudest before noon and softest after.

The librarian sometimes leaves a door unlocked that she usually keeps closed.

The ferry station master has one hour a day where he sits by the water and does nothing. He has been sitting there for twenty years. Nobody has ever sat with him.

**Maren can visit any location at any time.**

But what she finds there depends entirely on whether she was paying attention.

Rush through a conversation → the person finishes it politely and returns to their day. Nothing closes permanently. But the moment is gone.

Stay with someone past the point where it feels useful → they say something they hadn't planned to.

The game does not announce which moments matter.

It never will.

---

### The Mystery

There is no crime.

There is no flame going out.

The mystery is Maren herself.

She left something behind on the mainland. She has not spoken of it. The player discovers it sideways, through objects she carries, through conversations she deflects, through the one letter she has not sent.

The town slowly makes room for it.

Not by solving it.

By being present with her while she is present with it.

---

### The Philosophical Question

> What do you miss when you are always somewhere else?

Maren has spent her life being useful.

Useful is another word for *never fully arriving*.

The game asks: what is the cost of being mentally absent, even while physically present?

Not dramatically. Not as a lesson.

As a slow, ordinary accumulation of small moments she almost missed.

---

### The Answer

There is a conversation she has avoided for six months.

At the end of the game, she has it.

Not because the game forces her.

Because Varemoor has gradually taught her that the thing she was avoiding was not the conversation.

It was the act of stopping long enough to be in it.

The answer is not spoken.

It is in the quality of the silence that follows.

---

### Connection to Game I

The listening houses exist in Varemoor.

One of them is above the tea house. Maren walks past it every day without going in.

The last chapter is set inside it.

An old keeper passing through — not the Keeper of the flame, but a different sort of keeper, someone who knew someone who knew Asa — mentions that the boy from the stories used to say silence was never something to keep.

Maren says she isn't sure she knows the difference.

The keeper says: you've been learning it for six months. You just haven't noticed yet.

---

### Connection to Game IV

By the time Game IV takes place, the tea house has closed.

The listening house above it is the only thing still open.

The ferry station master — very old now, barely able to walk to the water — is the one person in Varemoor who stayed.

He remembers a woman named Maren who once sat with him without saying a word.

He has been waiting for someone to do that again ever since.

---

### Avoid

**Do not make rushing bad.**

The game must not punish haste.

Every schedule is still available, in some form, at some time.

Rushing only means the moment passes in a certain way.

A moment passed is still a moment lived.

The game should make the player *want* to slow down — not by punishing speed, but by making slowness so rich that speed feels like a cost the player chose.

**Do not make Maren a guide.**

She is not teaching the player anything.

She is finding something out herself.

The player is along for that.

**Do not explain mindfulness.**

The word "mindfulness" never appears.

Presence is never defined.

If the writing is right, the player will feel it. That is the only definition that matters.

**The Momo trap.**

Momo is already about stolen time and a girl who gives it back.

Game III is about time that is wasted willingly — not stolen.

Nobody takes Maren's attention.

She gives it away to busyness, to the next thing, to everything except the thing in front of her.

That is the modern version. The voluntary kind. The kind that needs no villain.

---

### Product Goal

Teach explorative reading.

Teach players to navigate a story — to choose what to attend to, knowing they cannot see everything.

The game is the first one where missing something is possible.

Not punishing.

But real.

A conversation you rushed past might have contained the thing you needed.

You won't know.

That is the point.

### Narrative Goal

Teach presence.

The noise of the modern world is not only sound.

It is the constant occupation of attention before you have decided to give it.

Maren's life has been a long series of things she did next.

This game is about the moment she learns to do *this* instead.

Players should finish feeling like they have been somewhere real.

Not that they have completed something.

Like they stayed long enough for it to matter.
